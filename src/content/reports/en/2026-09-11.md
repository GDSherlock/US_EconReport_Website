## Liquidity and high-frequency indicators

### Financial liquidity: Treasury cash drawdown drives the release

**Financial liquidity shifted from contraction to a marked expansion this week, led by lower TGA and reverse repo balances.** On September 9, financial liquidity stood at USD 5,459.905 billion, up USD 96.094 billion from September 2. Federal Reserve liabilities rose by USD 3.415 billion, adding liquidity; the TGA fell by USD 84.600 billion, releasing an equal amount; and reverse repos fell by USD 8.079 billion, also contributing positively. The Treasury cash drawdown accounted for most of the improvement, reinforced by reverse repos and a smaller contribution from Fed liabilities. The rebound was substantial after several weak weeks, but mostly reflected a one-week TGA movement. Its persistence will depend on what happens when Treasury cash is rebuilt.

### Short-term rates: stable funding, repriced expectations

**Short-term funding rates remained broadly stable, but the negative SOFR–OIS spread widened sharply as policy-rate expectations moved higher.** On September 11, SOFR was 3.64%, down 1 bp from September 4. OIS rose from 3.6525% to 3.8778%, an increase of 22.53 bp, while the spread widened from −0.25 bp to −23.77 bp. With SOFR almost unchanged, the movement was driven primarily by OIS. Spot financing conditions did not tighten materially, while pricing of the future policy path rose significantly. The central development in short-term conditions was therefore expectations repricing rather than funding stress.

### Real activity: resilient growth, softer consumption

**High-frequency activity signals diverged: growth momentum remained resilient while consumption growth moderated.** As of September 4, Redbook weekly retail sales growth fell from 9.6% to 8.3% year on year, a decline of 1.3 percentage points. The Weekly Economic Index rose from 3.02 to 3.27. The St. Louis Fed Financial Stress Index increased from −0.8518 to −0.7884, indicating slightly greater stress; the Chicago Fed National Financial Conditions Index fell from −0.560 to −0.564, still suggesting relatively loose overall conditions. The 30-year fixed mortgage rate rose from 6.66% to 6.71%. High-frequency growth measures did not indicate a clear weakening of the economy, but the marginal changes in consumption and housing finance warrant monitoring.

### Weekly employment: no clear increase in layoff pressure

**Weekly labor data remained broadly stable, with no clear rise in layoff pressure.** Initial claims fell from 207,000 to 206,000 in the week ending September 4. The latest continued claims, for August 28, declined from 1.775 million to 1.774 million. Both edged lower, suggesting layoffs remained low and re-employment pressure did not worsen further. Combined with the August payroll rebound and unemployment holding at 4.1%, the market appears to be experiencing recovery from a low base alongside moderate cooling, rather than rapid deterioration.

## The US economy

### August employment: recovery from a low base, not renewed overheating

**Payrolls rebounded sharply, but unchanged unemployment and moderate wage growth point to recovery rather than renewed overheating.**

> **Data**
>
> August payrolls rose by 162,000, well above July’s revised 21,000; June and July were revised up by a combined 55,000. Unemployment remained at 4.1%, participation edged up to 61.6%, and private nonfarm average hourly earnings rose 0.3% month on month and 3.1% year on year. Food services added 59,000 jobs, local government education 42,000 and manufacturing 16,000; information lost 23,000.

Employment improved markedly from July, but gains were concentrated in certain industries and unemployment did not decline. Upward revisions eased fears of an abrupt stall, yet average monthly gains over the past 12 months were only about 31,000, well below the expansion’s peak pace. Wage growth of 0.3% monthly and 3.1% annually indicates continuing pressure without a fresh acceleration. Together with only a low-base recovery in July vacancies and a declining hiring rate, this looks more like a temporary rebound after slower hiring than a broad resurgence in demand.

### August existing-home sales: more supply, demand constrained by high rates

**High rates continued to restrain homebuying, pushing sales to a 14-month low, while rising inventory shifted the balance further toward buyers.**

> **Data**
>
> August existing-home sales fell to an annualized 3.98 million, down 2.0% on the month and 1.2% on the year, the lowest since June 2025. Inventory rose 3.2% monthly to 1.62 million homes, up 5.9% annually. Months supply increased from 4.6 in July to 4.9. The median price rose 1.6% year on year to USD 429,100.

Continued sales weakness primarily reflects high mortgage rates suppressing demand, rather than a further tightening of supply. Inventory reached its highest since November 2019 and listing times lengthened, indicating easing supply constraints. Higher financing costs nevertheless offset the support from more available homes. The market is moving from low inventory supporting prices amid weak sales toward improving inventory with demand still constrained by rates. Housing’s near-term contribution to household demand and related consumption remains weak.

### August business confidence and supply chains: resilience meets cost constraints

**Business confidence softened while supply-chain pressure rose, leaving resilient demand alongside cost constraints.**

> **Data**
>
> The NFIB Small Business Optimism Index fell 1.1 points from 99.8 in July to 98.7 in August, still above its 52-year average of 98.0. The uncertainty index fell two points to 89 but remained well above its long-run average. The New York Fed GSCPI rose from about 0.94 in July to about 1.06 in August; the New York Fed explicitly reported an increase in global supply-chain pressure.

Lower small-business confidence did not signal a broad deterioration in operations; it mainly reflected softer expectations for the business environment. Labor quality and cost pressures eased somewhat, consistent with the payroll rebound without clear wage acceleration. Meanwhile, a positive and rising GSCPI suggests renewed disruption from transport, deliveries and global supply constraints. Domestic demand and employment remain relatively resilient, but business expectations have not strengthened in parallel, and supply-chain costs could still interrupt disinflation.

## Central bank policy

### Federal Reserve: neutral wait-and-see, with risks tilting hawkish

**The Fed maintained a broadly neutral wait-and-see signal this week, but risks tilted further toward tightening.** No FOMC meeting, minutes, projections or dot plot were released during September 8–11. The next meeting is September 15–16 and includes updated projections. The Fed entered its pre-meeting blackout on September 5, leaving no new rate-path guidance. August PPI rose 0.4% monthly and 5.4% annually, with goods prices up 1.1% monthly. Strong upstream pressure makes it harder to confirm a sustained return to 2% inflation while employment remains resilient, preserving both a hike and an unchanged-rate decision as possibilities for September.

There were no important new monetary policy speeches this week because the communications blackout covers September 5–17. Chair Kevin Warsh, Vice Chair Philip Jefferson, Vice Chair for Supervision Michelle Bowman, other governors, New York Fed President John Williams and voting regional presidents for 2026 issued no new policy statements. Earlier remarks therefore cannot substitute for this week’s signal. However, the New York Fed’s September 8 consumer survey showed one- and five-year inflation expectations unchanged at 3.6% and 3.0%, while three-year expectations fell to 3.2%. The mean probability of higher unemployment over the next year rose to 44.4%, the highest since April 2020, showing that inflation persistence and downside employment risks coexist.

There was no confirmed policy pivot, but the latest prices shifted the reaction function toward guarding against upside inflation. If August CPI shows renewed broadening of core or services inflation alongside resilient employment—162,000 payroll gains and 4.1% unemployment—the case for a September hike will strengthen. If CPI cools materially while employment and credit weaken further, maintaining the 3.50%–3.75% range becomes more reasonable. Renewed discussion of cuts would require more persistent disinflation and evidence of labor deterioration. The key pre-meeting release is August CPI on September 11, alongside any further tightening in yields and credit conditions. The overall direction changed little, but near-term action risk tilted further toward tightening.

### European Central Bank: another hike, watching energy transmission

**The ECB turned clearly more hawkish and raised rates again, while retaining a meeting-by-meeting approach.** On September 10, the Governing Council unanimously raised all three policy rates by 25 bp, taking the deposit facility rate to 2.50%, and reiterated data dependence without a preset path. August headline inflation rose from 2.9% to 3.3%, mainly as energy inflation increased from 10.3% to 14.3%. Core inflation fell from 2.5% to 2.4%, services from 3.3% to 3.0%, and second-quarter compensation per employee growth from 3.5% to 3.3%. The hike therefore mainly addresses the energy shock and second-round risks. Further tightening remains possible, but near-term decisions will likely depend on how that shock passes through.

### Bank of Japan: wages and costs support gradual normalization

**The BOJ continued to signal gradual hikes and normalization, with stronger wages and cost pressure reinforcing the case for further tightening this year.** Board member Kazuki Masu said on September 10 that Japan had escaped deflation, the 1.0% policy rate remained below the estimated neutral range, financial conditions were still accommodative and further hikes were needed. July cash earnings rose 4.7% annually, regular wages 4.1% and real wages 2.4%, their seventh consecutive positive month, reinforcing the wage-price cycle. August corporate prices were still up 7.6% annually. Masu also stressed that FX policy belongs to the government and Ministry of Finance: the BOJ does not set rates directly for the exchange rate, although yen depreciation now passes through more strongly to prices. Wages, the weak yen and costs support normalization, but its pace depends on the September assessment of inflation persistence.

## Key developments

### Middle East conflict: risks spread to shipping routes and energy transport

**The conflict spread beyond an ongoing standoff toward critical waterways and energy transport, lifting the global oil risk premium.** US–Iran military activity around tankers and maritime targets intensified. Iran-linked Houthi forces seized Yemen’s port of Mocha, creating risks to the Bab el-Mandeb–Red Sea route alongside restrictions in the Strait of Hormuz. Brent briefly reached USD 109.97 a barrel on Friday and gained nearly 13% over the week. The energy shock began transmitting through transport, refining and inflation expectations into bonds and equities. A prolonged closure of key routes is not yet established. Monitor actual traffic through Hormuz and Bab el-Mandeb, Saudi energy infrastructure security and any further military escalation.

### Global long bonds: energy, fiscal supply and term premia reprice together

**Long-term bonds faced another broad selloff as repricing extended beyond policy expectations to energy, fiscal supply and term premia.** The US 10-year yield briefly reached about 4.97%, approaching 5%, while the 30-year rose to about 5.38%, a nearly two-decade high. Markets remained cautious after the Treasury expanded long-bond buybacks. Japan’s Nikkei 225 and Korea’s KOSPI each briefly fell about 2.6% on Friday, showing how high energy and financing costs constrain richly valued assets, although positioning and valuations also contributed. Watch whether the 10-year yield decisively breaches 5%, the supply-demand balance for long bonds and the persistence of high oil prices.

### AI infrastructure: deeper ties between cloud providers and custom chips

**AI infrastructure competition shifted further toward close integration of cloud providers, custom chips and high-speed interconnects.** Qualcomm and Amazon announced a multigeneration AI data-center chip collaboration focused on inference and optical interconnect technology up to 1.6 Tbps. SEC documents show Amazon received warrants for up to 25 million Qualcomm shares, with vesting linked to purchase orders and actual purchases and an aggregate payment ceiling of USD 60 billion. This is not a fixed USD 60 billion order already secured. Qualcomm shares rose more than 3% following the news. The agreement reinforces cloud providers’ efforts to diversify compute suppliers and reduce inference costs, while extending AI capital expenditure into networking, optical communications and custom ASICs. Actual order conversion, project margins and scalable substitution remain the key tests.
