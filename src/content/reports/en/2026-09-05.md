## Liquidity and high-frequency indicators

### Financial liquidity: higher Treasury cash and reverse repos weigh on balances

**Financial liquidity returned to contraction this week, mainly because TGA and reverse repo balances rose.** On September 2, liquidity stood at USD 5,363.811 billion, down USD 12.525 billion from August 26. Fed liabilities increased by USD 6.258 billion, adding liquidity, while the TGA rose USD 17.199 billion and reverse repos USD 1.584 billion, absorbing liquidity in the same amounts.

Fed liability growth was insufficient to offset those withdrawals, reversing the prior week’s improvement. Liquidity was still USD 6.909 billion below August 19 and USD 26.401 billion below August 12. This looks more like a continuation of the recent contraction than a trend reversal following one week of volatility.

### Short-term rates: slightly tighter funding, expectations drive the spread

**Short-term rates edged higher, but the wider negative SOFR–OIS spread was mainly driven by OIS and reflected policy expectations repricing.** On September 4, SOFR was 3.65%, up 1 bp from August 28. OIS rose from 3.7435% to 3.7933%, an increase of 4.98 bp; the spread widened from −10.35 bp to −14.33 bp.

Because OIS rose much more than SOFR, the spread move was not primarily evidence of deteriorating spot financing conditions. It more strongly reflected repricing of the future policy path. Hawkish Fed remarks initially raised hike expectations; subsequent softer comments only partly offset them, leaving weekly OIS higher overall. The main shift in short-term conditions remained in expectations rather than clear funding stress.

### Real activity: high-frequency improvement and modest financial easing

**High-frequency real activity improved together, suggesting resilient growth, with only modest easing in financial conditions.** As of August 28, Redbook weekly retail sales growth rose from 9.1% to 9.6% year on year, up 0.5 percentage points. The Weekly Economic Index increased from 2.84 to 3.06, improving for a second week from 2.59 on August 14.

The St. Louis Fed Financial Stress Index declined from −0.8101 to −0.8526, and the Chicago Fed National Financial Conditions Index edged down from −0.556 to −0.558. Both indicate marginal easing. High-frequency consumption and activity have not shown a clear loss of momentum, but the improvement in financial conditions is limited and resilience still needs confirmation from employment and spending data.

### Weekly employment: marginal weakness without widespread layoffs

**Weekly employment data softened at the margin without pointing to large-scale corporate layoffs.** Initial claims rose from 204,000 to 206,000 in the week ending August 28. The latest continued claims, for August 21, increased from 1.771 million to 1.779 million.

The simultaneous increases suggest slightly greater layoff pressure and re-employment difficulty. Absolute levels nevertheless remain relatively stable, without evidence of concentrated employment cuts. July vacancies recovered from June’s low while the hiring rate fell further. The labor market therefore looks closer to a gradual cooling in recruitment demand than rapid deterioration.

## The US economy

### August ISM: services outperform manufacturing amid growth and price pressure

**Manufacturing expansion slowed at the margin while services demand strengthened further, preserving a services-led growth pattern with divergent employment and price signals.**

> **Data**
>
> Manufacturing PMI fell from 55.6 in July to 54.6 in August, remaining expansionary. New orders declined from 56.7 to 53.7, employment from 52.8 to 51.2 and production from 58.5 to 58.3; prices held at 71.1. Non-manufacturing PMI rose from 54.1 to 55.4. Business activity increased from 59.1 to 61.7, new orders from 57.2 to 60.9 and employment from 47.4 to 47.8, still below 50. Prices rose from 70.3 to 72.6.

Manufacturing continued to expand, but demand and hiring cooled. Production remained high while orders and employment eased, indicating a slower expansion. Services activity and orders accelerated significantly, yet employment remained contractionary, so stronger demand had not fully translated into hiring.

Price pressure remained pronounced. Manufacturing prices stayed elevated and services prices rose to 72.6, suggesting limited relief in business costs. Growth resilience alongside weak employment expansion and sticky prices will continue to complicate monetary policy decisions.

### July job openings: low-base recovery, weaker actual hiring

**Vacancies recovered from a low level, but actual hiring weakened further, leaving the labor-market cooling trend intact.**

> **Data**
>
> July openings totaled 7.271 million, up 89,000 from June’s revised 7.182 million but below May’s 7.537 million. Manufacturing added 79,000 openings, education and health services 52,000 and government 48,000. Professional and business services lost 65,000, leisure and hospitality 42,000, and trade, transportation and utilities 15,000. The openings rate rose from 4.3% to 4.4%, while the hiring rate fell from 3.4% to 3.2%.

Vacancies partly recovered after June’s marked decline, but willingness to hire remained weak. The total had not returned to May’s level, and industry divergence remained clear, indicating no broad improvement in labor demand.

The combination of a higher openings rate and lower hiring rate is especially relevant: firms retained staffing needs but became less willing to convert them into actual recruitment. Together with small increases in both initial and continued claims, the picture is one of gradual rebalancing through slower hiring and limited layoffs rather than concentrated job cuts.

### July trade: imports outpace exports and the deficit widens

**Annual growth in both exports and imports slowed, but imports remained faster, widening the trade deficit and increasing the marginal drag from net exports.**

> **Data**
>
> Export growth slowed from 12.91% to 9.34% year on year and import growth from 14.36% to 11.15%. The trade balance widened from −USD 71.182 billion in June to −USD 88.576 billion in July, a USD 17.394 billion increase in the deficit, also exceeding May’s −USD 75.752 billion.

June’s narrowing did not persist. Although both annual growth rates fell, imports continued to outpace exports, widening the deficit and creating a greater net-export drag on current real GDP.

August ISM manufacturing new export orders stood at 53.2 and imports at 52.5; services export orders and imports were also expansionary. Cross-border corporate demand therefore had not clearly contracted. July’s trade structure reflects both slower external demand growth and continuing resilience in US domestic demand.

## Central bank policy

### Federal Reserve: conditionally hawkish, with a hike less certain

**Fed signals were conditionally hawkish but softened at the margin: a September hike remained a possibility, not a predetermined action.** No FOMC meeting, minutes, projections or dot plot were released this week. The next policy meeting is September 15–16.

The September 2 Beige Book reported modest overall expansion, only slight employment growth and moderate wage gains. Prices continued to rise broadly, but eight of 12 districts reported similar increases, three slower increases and only one faster increases. Neither activity nor employment weakened enough to force easing, while inflation did not show a uniform reacceleration. The setting favored awaiting key data rather than committing early to a hike.

Officials clarified this conditional reaction function. At the G20 on August 31, Chair Warsh focused on AI and the global investment wave potentially raising productivity and potential growth, without new explicit September guidance. Governor Barr said on September 1 that growth was solid and employment stable, but inflation remained above target: decisive hikes would be appropriate if inflation failed to ease sufficiently, while continued movement toward 2% would allow a longer observation period.

New York Fed President Williams said on September 2 that recent inflation had improved but one or two months were insufficient to confirm the trend; September would depend on data and risks. Governor Waller said on September 3 that continued August inflation improvement would support holding the 3.50%–3.75% range, while renewed strength would warrant considering a hike. He viewed employment as near its maximum sustainable level and policy as only slightly restrictive, noting a marked fall in three-month annualized core PCE inflation. These remarks reduced the certainty of an immediate hike.

The Fed’s inflation-first, data-dependent direction remained intact, but strong hike warnings shifted toward more explicit conditional choices. Broader inflation, persistent core services pressure and resilient employment and demand could still prompt a September or later hike. Continued disinflation with stable employment would favor waiting. Cuts would re-enter discussion only with sustained inflation improvement and clear labor deterioration.

Before the meeting, focus on August payrolls, September 10 PPI and September 11 CPI, plus whether energy prices, long yields and overall financial conditions tighten further. Relative to the previous week, the policy direction changed little, but the certainty that September must bring a hike declined substantially.

### European Central Bank: stronger case for a hike, then a possible pause

**The ECB’s policy bias became more hawkish, strengthening the case for September tightening, but a pause to assess the energy shock may follow.** There was no formal meeting this week and no clear new rate guidance from major officials, leaving inflation and activity data as the main drivers.

August euro-area HICP inflation rose from 2.9% to 3.3%, led by energy inflation accelerating from 10.3% to 14.3%. Core inflation fell from 2.5% to 2.4% and services inflation from 3.3% to 3.0%. Composite PMI stood at 52.0 and services PMI at 51.6, with services input and output prices at three-month highs.

The energy shock lifted near-term headline inflation and strengthened the case for another tightening step. Falling core and services inflation, however, did not support a series of large hikes. One additional move followed by renewed data dependence and meeting-by-meeting decisions appeared more likely.

### Bank of Japan: stronger signals of gradual hikes

**The BOJ reinforced gradual hikes and normalization, raising the likelihood of September tightening, though weak consumption still constrained the pace.** Kazuo Ueda said rates would continue to rise while financial conditions remained accommodative, with September focusing on the baseline scenario and upside inflation risks. Hajime Takata favored adjusting policy flexibly with activity and prices rather than adhering to fixed intervals.

Takata’s materials showed real wages had turned positive and the 10-year breakeven inflation rate was elevated at around 2%, with the wage-price cycle improving. However, July real household spending fell 3.6% annually, its eighth consecutive contraction, indicating weak household demand. August consumer confidence edged up to 35.5.

The yen rebounded about 2.5% this week, although earlier weakness could still transmit to domestic prices through import costs. Intervention belongs to the Ministry of Finance; the BOJ continues to judge policy mainly through wages, inflation and demand. Wages and prices increasingly support normalization, but weak consumption means subsequent hikes are likely to remain gradual.

## Key developments

### US–Iran conflict: renewed risk in the Strait of Hormuz

**The conflict escalated again, leaving Hormuz among the most important geopolitical risks to global energy markets.** After a brief easing, the US resumed strikes on Iranian Revolutionary Guard air defense, radar, maritime and mine-laying capabilities. Iran responded with missile and drone attacks against US forces and Gulf-state targets, and said it would further restrict passage.

Only four merchant ships passed through Hormuz on September 1, well below the roughly 13-per-day average over the preceding ten days. Repricing of disruption risks lifted Brent 4.6% that day to USD 94.65 a barrel and WTI 5.2% to USD 90.22, while equities faced simultaneous pressure from higher oil and yields.

Watch whether either side further broadens military targets and whether actual shipping volumes recover. The first determines the potential increase in geopolitical premia; the second directly affects physical crude oil and LNG flows.

### AI industry: competition extends into model platforms and developers

**AI competition extended from compute chips into model distribution, development platforms and developer ecosystems, strengthening vertical integration.** Nvidia confirmed a USD 12.93 billion acquisition of Hugging Face, a major open-source AI model platform, including about USD 11.9 billion for investors and up to USD 1 billion for employee equity retention. It also committed to keeping the platform open without mandatory use of Nvidia chips or cloud services.

Beyond GPU hardware, the transaction brings Nvidia further into models, datasets and developer access, potentially strengthening its ecosystem position in inference and enterprise deployment. It may also intensify software compatibility and ecosystem competition for AMD, custom ASICs and other accelerator providers.

AI capital expenditure is expanding from compute capacity into a full stack of chips, models, platforms and development tools. Regulatory review, platform openness and continued hardware neutrality at Hugging Face are the key issues to monitor.

### Global trade policy: subsidy and export-surplus disputes intensify

**Coordination over industrial subsidies, excess exports and non-market policies intensified, raising the risk that constraints on Chinese manufactured exports spread beyond unilateral US measures.** The G20 finance ministers’ chair statement called for reducing non-market policies that worsen global imbalances and for economies with persistent large surpluses to rely less on export-led growth.

According to reports, participants other than China broadly supported the language, while China objected. US Treasury Secretary Bessent publicly urged other economies to consider trade barriers against Chinese goods.

No unified new tariffs or restrictions had emerged, but discussions broadened from bilateral friction to global capacity, subsidies and imbalances. Policy uncertainty consequently rose for export-intensive industries including autos, semiconductors, solar, steel and nonferrous metals. Watch whether individual economies convert G20 statements into restrictions, and whether high-level US–China dialogue eases disagreement.
